BCS 116: ELECTRICAL PRINCIPLES

Detailed Theory and Practice Notes

Electrical principles explain how electrical energy is generated, transmitted, controlled, and used. These principles are essential in understanding circuits, machines, power systems, and modern electronic devices.

1. The Nature of Electricity

Electricity refers to the movement and behavior of electric charges. It is one of the most important forms of energy used in human societies today. Most electrical devices depend on the controlled movement of charge through conductors.

Electrical systems are built around a few core ideas: current, voltage, resistance, power, and energy.

1.1 Electric Charge

Charge is a property of particles such as electrons and protons. Electrons carry negative charge and are the particles responsible for electric current in metals.

  • Like charges repel each other.
  • Unlike charges attract.
  • Charge is measured in coulombs (C).

1.2 Electric Current

Current is the rate at which charge flows through a conductor. It is measured in amperes (A).

I = Q / t

If 20C of charge passes through a wire in 5s, the current is 20/5 = 4A.

1.3 Electric Potential Difference (Voltage)

Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points. It drives current through a circuit.

V = W / Q

Voltage is measured in volts (V).

2. Resistance and Conductance

Resistance is the opposition to current flow in a material. Conductors have low resistance; insulators have very high resistance.

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

MaterialResistance Behavior
MetalsLow resistance
Wood, plastic, glassHigh resistance
SemiconductorsIntermediate resistance

2.1 Factors Affecting Resistance

The resistance of a conductor depends on:

  • length
  • cross-sectional area
  • material type
  • temperature

Increasing length increases resistance; increasing cross-sectional area decreases resistance.

3. Ohm’s Law

Ohm’s law states that the current in a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance.

V = I R

Equivalent forms:

I = V / R

R = V / I

3.1 Worked Examples

Example 1: If V = 12V and R = 4Ω, then I = 12 / 4 = 3A.
Example 2: If a circuit draws 2A and has 10V across it, resistance is 10 / 2 = 5Ω.

4. Circuit Basics

An electrical circuit is a continuous path through which current can flow. Every circuit consists of some combination of sources, conductors, load devices, and switching/control elements.

PartRole
SourceSupplies energy
ConductorsCarry current
LoadConsumes electrical energy
SwitchOpen or closes the path
Protection devicesPrevent damage

4.1 Closed and Open Circuits

  • Closed circuit: complete path, current flows
  • Open circuit: path broken, current does not flow

5. Series and Parallel Circuits

5.1 Series Circuit

Components are connected one after another in a single loop. The same current flows through each component.

R_total = R1 + R2 + R3

If R1 = 4Ω, R2 = 6Ω, R3 = 10Ω, then total resistance is 20Ω.

5.2 Parallel Circuit

Components are connected side by side across the same voltage source. The source voltage is the same across each branch.

1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3

If two resistors of 10Ω and 20Ω are in parallel, the equivalent resistance is smaller than both of them.

6. Power and Energy

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is converted or used.

P = V I

Equivalent forms:

P = I^2 R

P = V^2 / R

Time-based energy relation:

E = P t

6.1 Example

If a lamp uses 60W for 3 hours, then E = 60 × 3 = 180Wh = 0.18kWh.

Semiconductors are used in diodes, transistors, LEDs, and integrated circuits. They are the backbone of digital electronics and modern technology.

8.1 Fuse and Circuit Breaker

These devices protect electrical systems from overload and short circuits. They disconnect power when current becomes dangerous.

9. AC and DC

TypeKey FeatureExample
DCCurrent flows in one direction onlyBattery
ACDirection changes periodicallyMains electricity

Alternating current is easier to transmit over long distances and is commonly used in electrical grids.

10. Magnetism and Electromagnetism

Electric current produces magnetic fields. This relationship forms the basis of motors, generators, transformers, and many measurement devices.

When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, or when magnetic flux changes, an electromotive force is induced.

This principle is the basis of electromagnetic induction used in transformers and electric generators.

10.1 Transformers

Transformers transfer electrical energy between circuits using magnetic induction. They are essential for stepping voltage up or down.

11. Kirchhoff’s Laws

11.1 KCL

Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL): the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving it.

11.2 KVL

Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL): the algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero.

These laws are used to solve complex electrical networks and circuits.

12. Electrical Safety

Working with electricity can be dangerous. Safety measures protect people and equipment from shocks, burns, and fire.

  • Use insulated tools
  • Turn off power before repair
  • Use fuses and breakers
  • Do not overload sockets
  • Ensure correct grounding and earthing

15. Summary

Electrical principles explain how current, voltage, resistance, and power interact in circuits. These core ideas are essential for the design, analysis, and operation of electrical systems and modern electronic devices. Understanding them helps in solving practical engineering problems safely and effectively.
